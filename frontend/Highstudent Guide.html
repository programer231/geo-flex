Highstudent Guide

Understanding statistical tests on time series. A guide for high-school students: Earth data (rainfall, temperature, snow, river flow…)

The website analyses a variable (for example rainfall) over a chosen area and period, then applies several statistical tests. This guide explains, in plain words and with examples, what each test is looking for and how to read its result. No prior knowledge of statistics is needed: we start from the mean and from probability.

The basics in 15 minutes

A time series

Definition: time series

A time series is a sequence of measurements of the same quantity taken at successive dates: for example the total rainfall of each month since 1990.

A satellite or a weather station produces one value per day, per month or per year. The tests on the website answer questions about this sequence: Is it going up? Did something change in 2005? Is there a seasonal cycle?

Summarising a series: mean, median, standard deviation

For \(n\) values \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\):

  • the mean is \(\bar{x}=\dfrac{x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n}{n}\);

  • the median is the middle value once the data are sorted (half of the values are below it, half above);

  • the standard deviation \(\sigma\) measures the spread around the mean: a large \(\sigma\) means widely scattered values.

The coefficient of variation \(CV=\dfrac{\sigma}{\bar{x}}\times 100\) expresses the spread as a percentage of the mean. It allows a very variable quantity (rainfall, high CV) to be compared with a very regular one (temperature, low CV).

Example: the median resists extreme values

Rainfall over 5 months (mm): \(10,\;12,\;11,\;13,\;300\). The mean is \(69.2\) mm, but the median is \(12\) mm. A single flood pulled the mean upwards: the median describes a “typical” month better.

Slope and percentage

A trend is measured by a slope. For a line \(y=ax+b\), the number \(a\) is the slope: if \(x\) is time in years and \(y\) is temperature in \(^\circ\)C, then \(a=0.03\) means “\(+0.03\,^\circ\)C every year”, that is \(+0.3\,^\circ\)C in 10 years.

Null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis

A statistical test works like a trial. The accused is presumed innocent until the evidence is strong enough.

Definition: hypotheses \(H_0\) and \(H_1\)

  • \(H_0\), the null hypothesis, is the “nothing is happening” position: no trend, no break, normal data…

  • \(H_1\), the alternative hypothesis, is the opposite: “something is happening”.

We never prove \(H_0\). We only check whether the data are so surprising that \(H_0\) can no longer be believed.

The p-value

Definition: p-value

The p-value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming \(H_0\) is true. It is a number between 0 and 1.

Example: a rigged coin?

You toss a coin 10 times and get “heads” 10 times. If the coin is fair (\(H_0\)), the probability of getting the same side 10 times is \(2\times\left(\tfrac12\right)^{10}=\tfrac{2}{1024}\approx 0.002\). The p-value is therefore about \(0.002\) (0.2%): this is very unlikely with a fair coin, so we doubt \(H_0\).

The threshold \(\alpha\) and the decision

Before the test we fix a threshold, the significance level \(\alpha\), almost always \(\alpha=0.05\) (5%). The rule is the same for all the tests on the website:

Result Decision Sentence to use
\(p<0.05\) we reject \(H_0\) “Significant result: \(H_1\) is plausible”
\(p\geq 0.05\) we do not reject \(H_0\) “Not enough evidence”
Warning

\(p\geq 0.05\) does not mean “\(H_0\) is true”. It means “the data are not enough to conclude”. As in court: “not guilty for lack of evidence” is not “proven innocent”.

The two possible errors

\(H_0\) is actually true \(H_1\) is actually true
We reject \(H_0\) False alarm (type I error), with probability \(\alpha\) Correct decision
We do not reject \(H_0\) Correct decision Missed detection (type II error)

Significant does not mean important

With a lot of data, even a tiny trend becomes “significant”. So always look at two things: the p-value (is it real?) and the size of the effect, for example the slope in \(^\circ\)C per year (is it important?).

Key takeaway

  1. A test asks a question whose “default” answer is \(H_0\).

  2. \(p<0.05\): we reject \(H_0\); \(p\geq 0.05\): we cannot conclude.

  3. The p-value says whether an effect is real, not whether it is large.

Describing the series

Before any test, the website computes simple statistics: mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, coefficient of variation, and two indicators of shape.

  • Skewness: close to \(0\), the series is symmetric; positive, there are a few very large values (typical of rainfall); negative, a few very small ones.

  • Kurtosis: positive, extreme values are more frequent than usual.

Key takeaway

If the mean is very different from the median, or if the skewness is large, the series probably contains extreme values. This affects the choice of tests (next section).

Testing normality

Question: do the data have the shape of a “bell” (normal distribution)?

Definition: normal distribution

In a normal distribution, most values are close to the mean and very distant values are rare, symmetrically. The graph is the bell curve of Figure 1.

Why it matters: some tests (mean comparison, regression, Welch’s t-test) are reliable mainly when the data are roughly normal. Otherwise we prefer non-parametric tests (Mann-Kendall, Sen, Spearman, Mann-Whitney), which work on ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) and resist extreme values.

The website uses three tests (Shapiro-Wilk, D’Agostino, Jarque-Bera). For all of them, \(H_0\) = “the data are normal”.

  • \(p<0.05\): the data are not normal;

  • \(p\geq 0.05\): nothing prevents us from treating them as normal.

Two plots help to check: the histogram (compared with a bell) and the Q-Q plot: if the points follow the line, the data are normal.

Warning

Rainfall is almost always non-normal (many small values, a few huge ones). The result “not normal” is therefore expected, which is why the website favours non-parametric tests.

Stationarity

Definition: stationary series

A series is stationary if its properties (mean, spread) do not drift over time. A series with a strong trend is not stationary.

The website uses two tests that ask the question in opposite ways:

  • ADF: \(H_0\) = “non-stationary”. \(p<0.05\) \(\Rightarrow\) stationary.

  • KPSS: \(H_0\) = “stationary”. \(p<0.05\) \(\Rightarrow\) non-stationary.

ADF KPSS Conclusion
\(p<0.05\) \(p\geq 0.05\) Stationary (strong evidence)
\(p\geq 0.05\) \(p<0.05\) Non-stationary (strong evidence)
\(p<0.05\) \(p<0.05\) Contradictory results: be careful
\(p\geq 0.05\) \(p\geq 0.05\) Not enough data to conclude
Key takeaway

Two tests are better than one: when they disagree, we assert nothing.

Autocorrelation

Definition: autocorrelation

Autocorrelation measures how much a value resembles the previous value. It ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\).

Example: temperature

After a hot month, the next month is more likely to be hot too: the values are not independent, the autocorrelation is positive.

Why it matters: tests often assume that every measurement brings new information. Interviewing 100 people from the same family gives less information than 100 randomly chosen people: with autocorrelation, 120 months “are worth” less than 120 independent measurements. If we ignore this, we find too many “significant” trends.

The website computes the lag-1 autocorrelation (between a month and the next) and the Ljung-Box test (\(H_0\): no autocorrelation; \(p<0.05\): there is some). The ACF/PACF plots show the autocorrelation for each lag; bars that go beyond the blue band are significant.

Detecting a trend

Question: does the variable increase or decrease over time?

Figure 3. The values fluctuate, but overall they rise: this is a trend.

The Mann-Kendall test

Idea: we compare each value with all the following ones and count: \(+1\) if the later value is larger (increase), \(-1\) if it is smaller (decrease), \(0\) if equal. The sum of these points is called \(S\).

  • \(S\) very positive: more increases than decreases \(\Rightarrow\) upward trend;

  • \(S\) very negative: downward trend;

  • \(S\) close to \(0\): no clear trend.

Example: computing \(S\) by hand

Series: \(3,\;4,\;6,\;5,\;8\). We compare each value with the following ones.

Value Compared with the next ones Points
\(3\) with \(4,6,5,8\): \(+,+,+,+\) \(+4\)
\(4\) with \(6,5,8\): \(+,+,+\) \(+3\)
\(6\) with \(5,8\): \(-,+\) \(0\)
\(5\) with \(8\): \(+\) \(+1\)
Total \(S\) \(\mathbf{+8}\)

There are \(10\) pairs in total, so \(S\) is at most \(10\). We compute \(\tau=\dfrac{S}{10}=0.8\): a clear upward trend. (With only 5 values the test is not reliable: aim for at least 10 values, and ideally many more.)

The website converts \(S\) into a statistic \(Z\) and then into a p-value. Just remember: \(|Z|>1.96\) is the same as \(p<0.05\), and the sign of \(Z\) gives the direction (positive = increase, negative = decrease). The coefficient \(\tau\) (Kendall’s tau, between \(-1\) and \(+1\)) measures how clear the trend is.

Sen’s slope: by how much does it change?

Mann-Kendall says whether there is a trend, not by how much. Sen’s slope computes the slope between every pair of points, then takes the median of all these slopes.

Example: Sen’s slope for \(3,4,6,5,8\)

The 10 slopes (change in value divided by change in time), sorted, are \(-1;\;0.5;\;0.67;\;1;\;1;\;1.25;\;1.33;\;1.5;\;2;\;3\). The median is the average of the 5th and 6th: \(\dfrac{1+1.25}{2}=1.125\) per time step. The values \(-1\) and \(3\) (extremes) have almost no effect.

The website gives the slope per year, with a 95% confidence interval.

  • If the interval does not contain \(0\) (for example \([0.12,\,0.48]\)), the trend is significant.

  • If it contains \(0\) (for example \([-0.10,\,0.30]\)), we cannot rule out a zero slope.

The website also reports the total change over the period, as a percentage of the mean.

The other trend tests

  • Modified Mann-Kendall (Hamed-Rao): the same test, corrected for the effect of autocorrelation. The report shows a variance factor: the larger it is, the more autocorrelation was weakening the classical test. If the two results differ, we keep the modified one.

  • Seasonal Mann-Kendall: for monthly data, Mann-Kendall is applied separately to each month (all Januaries, then all Februaries…) and the results are combined: the seasons can therefore not be mistaken for a trend.

  • Linear regression (OLS): we draw the line that passes as close as possible to the points. It gives a slope and an \(R^2\) (the share of variation explained by time, between 0 and 1). It is sensitive to extreme values and assumes roughly normal data.

  • Spearman correlation (values against time): measures whether the rank of a value increases with time (between \(-1\) and \(+1\)).

Key takeaway

A trend is convincing when several tests agree (modified Mann-Kendall, Spearman, regression), Sen’s slope is consistent with them, and its confidence interval does not contain \(0\).

Detecting a break

Question: did an abrupt change occur at some date? (station moved, instrument changed, climatic event…)

Live example (fictional rainfall, mm): the Pettitt test is computed in your browser.

Figure 4. The average level changes abruptly around 2005 (fictional data).

The website uses four tools. For the first three, \(H_0\) = “the series is homogeneous (no break)”.

  • Pettitt: we cut the series at every possible date and look for the one where the two pieces differ the most. It also gives the probable date of the break.

  • SNHT: compares the mean before and after each date, then keeps the largest difference. The p-value is obtained by simulation: the computer creates thousands of random series without a break and checks how exceptional the observed difference is.

  • Buishand: we accumulate the deviations from the mean. Without a break, this running total stays near \(0\); with a break, it drifts far to one side before coming back.

  • von Neumann ratio: close to \(2\) for a random series; clearly different from \(2\) suggests dependence or a break. It has no p-value here.

Comparing before and after

If a break is found, the website compares the two periods:

  • Welch’s t-test: are the means different? (assumes fairly normal data)

  • Mann-Whitney: are the values of one period systematically larger? (non-parametric)

  • Levene: has the variability changed?

For all of them, \(p<0.05\) means “the two periods are different”.

Key takeaway

A break is credible when Pettitt, SNHT and Buishand point to about the same date. You must then look for a real explanation (a break can be a measurement problem, not a natural phenomenon).

Seasonality

Question: does the variable follow an annual cycle?

  • Kruskal-Wallis compares the 12 months: \(H_0\) = “all months look alike”. \(p<0.05\): at least one month differs, there is seasonality.

  • STL decomposition: the series is separated into three pieces, \[\text{series} \;=\; \text{trend} \;+\; \text{season} \;+\; \text{residual (the rest)}.\] The website gives a seasonal strength and a trend strength between \(0\) and \(1\): close to \(1\), the component dominates; close to \(0\), it is weak.

Example: Morocco

In a Mediterranean climate, rainfall is higher in winter than in summer: Kruskal-Wallis gives a tiny p-value, and the seasonal strength is high.

Relationship between two variables

Question: do two variables (for example rainfall and river flow) evolve together?

  • Pearson (\(r\), from \(-1\) to \(+1\)): linear link. \(+1\): rise together; \(-1\): one rises when the other falls; \(0\): no linear link.

  • Spearman and Kendall: monotonic link, computed on ranks. More robust to extreme values.

  • Lagged correlation: one series is shifted in time to see whether one precedes the other. Example: river flow reacts to rainfall 2 months later, so the best lag is \(+2\).

  • Granger: do past values of \(X\) help to predict \(Y\)?

Warning

Correlation is not causation. Ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer without one causing the other: the heat explains both. A “Granger” result only means “useful for prediction”, not “real cause”.

Reading a complete report

Here is a fictional extract of a report, for monthly rainfall (mm):

Mann-Kendall            stat=-2.91  p=0.0036 -> decreasing
Modified MK (Hamed-Rao) stat=-1.80  p=0.0719 -> no trend (variance factor=2.60)
Sen's slope (per year)  -0.48  95% CI [-0.81, -0.12] mm/year
Pettitt                 stat=1450   p=0.0210 -> BREAK (at 2005-04-01)

Reading.

  1. The classical Mann-Kendall sees a decrease (\(p<0.05\)) …

  2. … but the modified test no longer confirms it (\(p=0.0719>0.05\)): the factor \(2.60\) shows strong autocorrelation that was exaggerating the first conclusion. We keep the modified test.

  3. Sen’s slope (\(-0.48\) mm/year) is negative, but its interval only just excludes \(0\) while the modified test is not significant: we must stay cautious.

  4. Pettitt detects a break in April 2005 (\(p=0.021\)): it is wise to confirm it with SNHT and Buishand, then look for a possible cause.

Honest conclusion: we cannot claim a gradual decrease in rainfall, but a change of level around 2005 is likely.

Warning

Classic pitfalls

  • Too many tests: with \(20\) tests at 5%, the probability of getting at least one false alarm is \(1-0.95^{20}\approx 64\%\). A single isolated “significant” result is not proof.

  • Series too short: over fewer than \(10\) years, tests lack power.

  • Missing data: gaps can create false trends or false breaks.

  • Pixel too large: a small area seen by a low-resolution satellite is represented by only a few pixels.

Checklist.

  1. Is the period long enough, without large gaps?

  2. What is the unit of the variable (mm, \(^\circ\)C, m\(^3\)/s)?

  3. Are the data normal? If not, read the non-parametric tests.

  4. Is there autocorrelation? If so, read the modified Mann-Kendall.

  5. Do several tests agree?

  6. Is the effect large (slope, percentage) or only significant?

  7. Is a real cause plausible?

Exercises

Your turn

  1. A test gives \(p=0.03\) with \(\alpha=0.05\). What do we decide about \(H_0\)?

  2. Mann-Kendall gives \(p=0.20\) and Sen’s slope is \(-0.5\) mm/year. Is there a significant trend?

  3. ADF: \(p=0.40\); KPSS: \(p=0.01\). Is the series stationary?

  4. Compute \(S\) and \(\tau\) for the series \(10,\;9,\;8,\;6,\;5\).

  5. We run 20 tests at \(\alpha=0.05\) on purely random data. How many false alarms do we expect on average?

  6. Sen’s slope of a monthly series is \(0.0025\,^\circ\)C per month. What is the slope per year? What is the change over 30 years?

  7. Does a Pearson coefficient of \(r=0.85\) between rainfall and river flow prove that rainfall causes the flood?

Answers

  1. \(0.03<0.05\): we reject \(H_0\) (significant result).

  2. No: \(p=0.20\geq 0.05\). The observed slope may be due to chance.

  3. ADF does not reject (no evidence of stationarity) and KPSS rejects (non-stationary): the series is non-stationary.

  4. All pairs are decreases: \(S=-(4+3+2+1)=-10\), \(\tau=-10/10=-1\): a perfect decrease.

  5. \(20\times 0.05=1\) false alarm on average.

  6. \(0.0025\times 12=0.03\,^\circ\)C per year; over 30 years: \(0.9\,^\circ\)C.

  7. No: correlation is not causation (even if the physical link is plausible here, it must be justified by something other than \(r\)).
